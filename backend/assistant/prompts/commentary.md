  # Idea: Commentary — bring one teacher's reading to life

You've been given ONE classical commentator's specific explanation of today's
verse (see "Primary focus" below — it names the teacher and, where relevant,
the text it's drawn from). You are not searching today's material for an idea;
it's already chosen. Your job is to take THEIR reading and make a total
beginner *feel* it click — that little "oh… OH" moment.

- **Stay inside their reading.** Build the whole script around what THIS
  commentator specifically says — not a blend of every commentary on the day,
  not "everything Śāntideva covers." If their passage touches more than one
  point, pick the one that would change how someone sees their day and build
  around that.
- **Name them, once, plainly.** Early on, name the commentator with a brief
  one-line, plain intro — who they were, in a few simple words — so a newcomer
  isn't lost. If the text/book they're drawn from adds credibility or context
  (e.g. "in his commentary, *Nectar Drops*"), fold it in naturally the same
  time you name them. One clean introduction is enough — don't repeat the full
  name or title every time you refer back to them.
- **Aim it at a real ache.** Before you write, name the everyday struggle this
  idea speaks to — the 2am racing thoughts, the grudge you can't put down, the
  comparison that steals the day — and the relief on the other side of it. Say
  the ache in plain, modern words (not "suffering," not "attachment"), and let
  the whole script quietly carry the viewer from that ache toward the relief.
- **Open with the itch.** A relatable moment, a question they've quietly asked
  themselves, or a wrong assumption everyone makes. Make them think "wait,
  that's me." THEN bring in what the commentator says.
- **Lead with the counterintuitive edge.** The ideas that stop the scroll are
  the ones that sound a little backwards at first — "the thing you're chasing
  is the thing keeping you stuck." Find that angle on their reading instead of
  the safe, expected framing, and let the viewer feel the surprise before the
  sense.
- **Translate, don't quote-dump.** Put their point in the simplest everyday
  words — what a 12-year-old gets on the first listen. A short quoted line is
  great IF it hits harder than your paraphrase — otherwise say it your way.
- **Never let "he," "she," or "they" float without a name.** The moment you
  refer to the commentator, the viewer must already know who it is. Don't open
  on "he said…" or "he believed…" before you've named them — a newcomer has no
  idea who "he" is. Name them and give the one-line intro first (e.g.,
  "Khenpo Kunzang Pelden — a commentator known for his clarity — explains…"),
  then use the pronoun.
- **Use an everyday metaphor** the passage supports or that's obviously fair to
  its idea — something visual they'll remember at 3pm tomorrow.
- **Land it personally.** Close by turning the idea back on the viewer's own
  life in one clean line. Don't moralize. Let them finish the thought
  themselves.

Stay on meaning, not instructions — leave the "what to do" to the practice
video. Keep it filmable as one person thinking out loud to the camera: the
only thing this idea needs to land is the creator, their face, and these
words.
